# Getting Better at Decision Making in Yu-Gi-Oh!

Author: Joshua Schmidt.
Source: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wADwkzxtZL4>

This is the first episode of a planned series
that breaks "how to get good at Yu-Gi-Oh" into short single-topic videos,
rather than one huge one. This episode's topic is decision making.

## Summary

The video argues that in modern Yu-Gi-Oh almost everyone already has a good deck,
so what separates players who consistently do well is technical play and decision making,
not deck choice. The core claim: understanding your opponent's decks
is at least as important as understanding your own.
The rest is a set of principles for making better in-game decisions:

1. **Decision making, not deck choice, is the differentiator**
   good deck lists are cheap and everywhere, so nearly everyone brings one.
   The edge left is how you play, which leans more on knowing the opponent's deck
   than on knowing your own.
2. **No universal shortcut rules** the common question
   "where do I Ash / Imperm this deck?" asks for a shortcut that does not exist.
   Even the classic "Ash the Branded Fusion" has many game states where it is wrong.
   Shortcuts are a fine baseline when you are low on time against an unfamiliar deck,
   but not a path to optimal play.
3. **Avoid results-oriented thinking** a decision can be right even when it loses,
   and wrong even when it wins. Judge it by the information and odds you had
   at the moment, then stand by it (or honestly revise) afterward.
4. **Knowing the opponent's options makes most decisions logical**
   if you knew exactly what your opponent could do, you would not need to ask where to Ash.
   The threat of a card depends on what else the opponent could chain or has set up
   (Dracotail Lukias into Faimena, Rahu Dracotail into Urgula versus your Solemn).
5. **Know whole decks, not just single cards** their lines, what they want to open,
   and their typical non-engine. Some of this (extra deck, cards still in deck)
   can only be learned before the game, never inferred mid-duel
   (Backup @Ignister in Maliss, Orcust playing Dominus Impulse,
   Dracotail's set-card reads, the layered threat of Lunalight Fusion).
6. **Three ways to acquire this knowledge**: theory (read every deck's cards and extra deck),
   practice (play the decks yourself to feel where interruption hurts),
   and watching or discussing with someone who plays the deck.
   It is a lot of work, but it is the only route to consistently optimal decisions.

Takeaway: you cannot control whether every decision works out,
only that each one is made with a real understanding of what the opponent's deck can do.
To improve, learn not just your own deck but every deck
you could realistically face in a tournament.

### Decision making is the differentiator

Finding a good deck is easier than ever: tournament-topping lists for every play style
are freely available and card prices are low.
But everyone at tournaments has a good deck,
so what separates the people who do well from those who don't is the decisions they make.
There are small edges in deck building and deck choice
(that is the gap between making top cut and winning a YCS),
but consistently doing well is mostly technical play, decision making, and a little luck.
Understanding your own deck is only half the equation, if not less.
Understanding your opponent's decks is even more important.

### There are no shortcut rules

A common question is "where do I Ash this deck? Where do I Imperm this deck?"
Those are shortcuts: fine for a quick baseline when you are low on time
against a rogue deck you have never faced,
but if you want to do as well as possible, they don't really exist.
There is no rule in Yu-Gi-Oh that always applies.

Even the most classic interaction, "Ash the Branded Fusion", breaks constantly.
Against Branded Dracotail there are many game states
where the Branded Fusion is not the most threatening card,
and you would rather hold Ash for a Rahu or Ketu Dracotail.
Sometimes it is still correct to Ash the Branded Fusion, depending on the game state.
Even against pure Branded there are spots where you Ash Aluber the Jester of Despia instead:
mid-game, if they summon an Aluber with a loaded graveyard
and could search a Branded in Red to win, you may have to Ash the Aluber,
because a smart opponent plays around the Branded Fusion.
These calls rely on knowledge of the opponent's deck.

### Right decisions can lose, wrong decisions can win

Decisions can be correct even when they don't work out,
and incorrect even when they do. This is results-oriented thinking.
Impermanencing something and having it work does not make it correct;
Impermanencing something they happened to dodge does not make it wrong.
Example: the opponent summons a Vanquish Soul Razen and you Imperm it,
knowing they could have Vanquish Soul Heavy Borger or Vanquish Soul Caesar Valius,
but judging the chance low enough to be worth it.
They have it and you lose: that does not automatically make the play wrong.
Judge the decision by the odds at the time, then stand by it or honestly revise it later.

### Knowing the opponent's options makes decisions logical

If you truly understand every card in play on both sides,
most Yu-Gi-Oh decisions are logical, and you would not need to ask where to Ash.

Dracotail Lukias: with an Infinite Impermanence (or any targeted negation) in hand,
you have to consider they could chain a Dracotail Faimena.
A new player who doesn't know Faimena exists would just Imperm the Lukias.
But Impermanencing the Lukias is not automatically wrong:
if the opponent has the Lukias and only one other card,
they cannot chain Faimena, so the Imperm is completely safe.
The blanket rule "never Imperm a Lukias because they could have Faimena"
does not give you that knowledge.

Rahu Dracotail into Solemn Judgment:
if you don't know the deck and let the Rahu resolve,
they will usually send Dracotail Urgula to the graveyard, which pops your Solemn Judgment.
Now you have neither negated the Rahu (you were saving Solemn for something else)
nor kept your Solemn, punished for not knowing Urgula existed.
The same pattern applies to any deck that searches, sends from deck to graveyard,
or summons from the extra deck: being aware of the opponent's options at each moment
is what lets you make optimal decisions.

A caution on logic: people misuse it by reasoning
"because X could happen, I should not do Y",
which (like the Lukias case) is not always true.

### Know whole decks, not just single cards

Often the knowledge you need is entire decks: their strategies, lines,
and what they want to open with.

Backup @Ignister in Maliss: read for the first time, "add one, discard one"
doesn't sound worth an Ash Blossom, and you might save the Ash for a bigger target.
But if you know Maliss doesn't want to normal summon Backup @Ignister
(a subpar opener) and lacks many other Ash targets these days,
Ashing that normal summon at the start of the turn is usually correct.

Deck-building knowledge you can only get before the game:
Orcust can play Dominus Impulse, which you won't discover until you have already played into it,
because you cannot infer it from seeing a few dark monsters
(the light monsters that enable it never hit the board).
You also need to know whether a deck is typically played with hand traps or board breakers,
and how much non-engine space it even has.
Against a deck notoriously short on non-engine space,
it may not make sense to play around an Infinite Impermanence.

Dracotail is a good example: it wants to play mostly monsters,
so in game one a set card is unlikely to be an Imperm and more likely one of its three traps.
If they set one or two from deck and one from hand,
that hand-set card is a Dracotail Sting, Horn, or Flame you haven't seen yet:
knowing this sharpens every decision.

Lunalight shows how layered a single read can be. It is a punishing, polarizing tier-two deck
(they either summon a Lunalight Liger Dancer and usually win, or they don't and usually lose),
but you don't face it often, so few players have the practice.
Lunalight Fusion reads terrifying, but you must evaluate it in context:
Liger Dancer needs four monsters, so if they have fewer than three unknown cards
that could be Lunalight monsters, Lunalight Fusion cannot make Liger Dancer.
That does not mean you always let it resolve:
it can still make a Lunalight Perfume Dancer to search a Luna Light Perfume, which is real value.
Its threat also shifts with what they have already used that turn,
because Lunalight monsters have graveyard effects when used as fusion material
(Lunalight Silver Hound, Yellow Marten, Emerald Bird, Black Sheep, and Kaleido Chick).
And Lunalight Silver Hound is a graveyard spell/trap negation,
so letting Lunalight Fusion resolve with Silver Hound already in the graveyard
risks your own Dracotail Flame getting negated later.
There is no way to make that one call properly without understanding the deck.

### How to acquire this knowledge

Understanding everything else in the format is the hardest, most work-intensive,
and most important part of Yu-Gi-Oh, and probably matters more
than understanding your own deck. There are three ways:

- **Theoretical**: read all the cards and decks in the format,
  including what sits in their deck and extra deck.
  This is the only way to know hidden information before it is revealed,
  and a fine starting point, but limited in how much it can teach you.
- **Practical**: play the decks yourself.
  Josh learned far more about Lunalight once he actually played it,
  because you feel where interruption really hurts.
  When you play a deck, you notice "if I get Ashed here, that hurts",
  and you recognize those choke points next time you face it,
  making decisions with a conscious understanding of the deck.
- **Watching and discussing**: if you can't play the deck yourself,
  borrow it from a friend or testing partner, or sit with someone who plays it,
  run a few games, and actually talk through where to interrupt.

It is a lot of work, and you have to do it for many decks,
but it is the only way to make optimal decisions consistently.
Not every rogue deck, but at least everything you could realistically face
in a tournament, you should understand.
