### Intro

When we last left off, the game had just seen its most impactful shakeup ever
with the release of Duelist Alliance and the start of the Arc-V era.
Shaddoll and Burning Abyss would overnight shift what the expectation was
for a meta archetype to be able to achieve using its in-archetype pieces,
providing both power and recursion between their bosses and power play cards.
Qliphort was also recently introduced near the end of 2014
to provide the first meta pendulum strategy,
utilizing the mechanic to provide tribute fodder for their bosses,
with other new archetypes like Tellarknight just outside of the meta,
unfortunately failing due to the shift in the game's design as a whole.
With that said, there would be some clear outliers in power with this shift in design,
and more specifically a single card that made certain matchups completely unbalanced,
so a ban list update would be put in place right after the start of the year.

#### Ban list (January 1st)

On January 1st, and while there would only be a couple of hits,
the ban list would absolutely be filled with cards returning to the game
for the first time in years.
Newly banned would be `Super Polymerization`,
as the card being completely unrespondable was proving to be a serious problem
in the case of Shaddoll specifically,
as it could effectively delete any boss monster from the field in archetype,
as well as being a blowout side deck card going into the Shaddoll matchup.
Newly limited were `Artifact Moralltach`, being the power play of the Artifact engine,
`Dark Strike Fighter`, returning from the banned section with a main phase 1 lock
and a hard once per turn, cratering its power level but still giving it a place
in certain strategies, and `Snatch Steal`,
which set off every alarm in the community with its return.
Understand that a permanent monster steal was a massive power swing,
especially for the time period,
and as such would immediately move from here to be a staple card
in literally every deck of the format, which we'll see very soon.
Newly semi-limited, and all returning from one, were `Honest`, `Goyo Guardian`
and `Dark Hole`, all seeing decreased usage in the meta,
with Dark Hole turning heads initially
but being disregarded due to the graveyard triggers of almost every meta deck.
Lastly, unlimited were `Blackwing - Gale the Whirlwind`, `Mermail Abyssgunde`,
`Reborn Tengu`, `Reasoning` and `The Transmigration Prophecy`,
all seeing themselves to varying degrees being power crept by the modern metagame,
though some here would find new homes very soon.

All in all, this list only had two hits, both seemingly targeted at Shaddoll,
which was interesting, as by recent results Burning Abyss had already overtaken
Shaddoll's top deck position,
which would lead most players to closely watch the next couple of releases
to see if Burning Abyss would run away with the format,
or if a new threat would give them a true challenge.

### Secrets of Eternity

- Release date: January 16th, 2015
- Set type: core set
- Major strategies: Infernoid, Qliphort, Burning Abyss
- Impact: minor buffs and a preview of what's to come

Secrets of Eternity would be the first set of 2015,
and though many of its pieces would see play over the remainder of the era,
a lot of what was introduced here would take some time to actually see major success.

The new archetype in question here was Infernoid,
a series of FIRE Fiend monsters that all share the same summon condition
of banishing a number of Infernoids from hand or grave to summon themselves
while the total level of Infernoids you control is eight or lower,
with the level fours and lowers taking one, the five through eights taking two,
and the nine and ups taking three.
All of the eight and lowers also share a similar effect
to on quick effect tribute a monster to banish a card from the opponent's grave,
with each also having their own unique effect:
Antra bouncing an opponent's card, Harmadik popping a monster,
Patrulea popping a spell trap, Piaty hand ripping on battle damage dealt,
Seitsemas banishing a card at the end of the battle phase that it attacks in,
and Attondel able to attack twice if it destroys something in battle.
The most unique of the bunch, though, was undoubtedly Onuncu,
a level 10 that nukes all monsters other than itself on summon
and can tribute a monster to negate a spell or trap and banish it once per turn.
They also received a couple of Void spell traps to support the archetype,
with the only relevant one being `Void Seer`,
able to make one Infernoid unaffected by opponent's effects that turn,
and could be banished from grave to keep one alive.
Once this archetype clearly had a lot going for it in the current meta landscape,
"DD Crow on most bodies", able to swarm themselves off the previous waves' corpses,
and varied forms of removal and interaction,
but the major downside was that while they could swarm over and over again easily,
getting that first swarm out was extremely resource intensive
because of their summon conditions.
You either had to banish your entire opening to set up decently,
or relied on supplemental engines like Lightsworn to set up the grave decently enough
to make an early push,
which then would get shot in the foot simply by their restriction
of only being able to get out one boss monster,
as Onuncu would nuke your other pieces on summon
and, by its level being 10, lock you from summoning the other bodies
to use its tribute effect with.
Because of this, Infernoid would be relegated to the rogue level for now,
but would absolutely be an archetype to keep an eye on moving forward into the era.

Qliphort would see a secondary wave in `Qliphort Monolith`,
the other vanilla scale, able to draw one for each Qli you tributed in the end phase,
`Qliphort Stealth`, who on tribute summon bounces a card, locking responses from the opponent,
and `Re-qliate`, a temporary negate of any level four or lower normal summon
or a five or higher special summon, which in turn would reset the Qli stat lines.
Each of these would find their home in the current version of Qli,
with Monolith being standardly a one-of high scale searchable with `Summoner's Art`,
Stealth being a way to clear the opponent's board without responses
by tributing over Helix and Carrier,
and Re-qliate would see play effectively as a way to temporarily stun players
while setting up your own stat lines, while keeping the immunity effects of your Qli.

Burning Abyss would receive its third wave of TCG exclusive support,
being `Farfa, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss`,
able to temporarily banish a monster on field until the end phase,
`Libic, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss`,
able to summon a level three Fiend from hand with its effects negated,
`Cagna, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss`,
able to send a Burning Abyss spell trap from deck to grave,
`Malacoda, Netherlord of the Burning Abyss`, a ritual who was too bad to even matter,
and its ritual spell `Good & Evil in the Burning Abyss`,
which had the added effect of being able to banish itself from grave
to send a BA from hand to grave and search a BA card from deck to hand.
This wave would do a lot for the deck by being better effects on the whole
than the previous waves, although the new boss was borderline unplayable
by being a ritual monster.
However, thanks to Cagna specifically,
Good and Evil would actually find itself teched into some BA builds,
as both a way to trigger the hand BAs and search for Fire Lake from the deck,
which was unquestionably the best BA spell trap.

There were also three more archetypes introduced here,
all seemingly out of place as they lack the remainder of their archetypes,
being that of Yosenju, Nekroz and Ritual Beast.
As this set came out in the OCG after the next pack we were set to receive did so,
as such, these pieces will be set aside to talk about in an upcoming section.

On to the one-offs.
`Performapal Partnaga` was a three scale that could boost a Performapal from the scale,
but more importantly was a three scale that was searchable
with the previously released `King of the Feral Imps`,
a point that would become more and more relevant over the coming months.
Similarly, `Gigobyte` was another King target
that could special summon itself if you control a Spellcaster
and floats into a different named 1500 and 200 stat monster on destruction,
also seeing relevance later in the year.
`Uni-Zombie` could, by discarding a card or dumping a Zombie from deck to grave,
boost a monster's level by one, once per turn for each method,
being a critical setup piece for Zombie strategies.
`Gem-Knight Lady Lapis Lazuli` was a fusion of the vanilla Lapis and another Gem-Knight,
able to dump a Gem-Knight from deck or extra
to burn the opponent for each special summoned monster on the field,
only being able to be special summoned once per turn to avoid burn loops,
but given time, Gem-Knight could find a way around that restriction.
`Stellarknight Constellar Diamond` was a three material rank five
that could also be summoned over a Tellarknight Xyz in the main phase 2,
blocking sending cards from deck to grave
and banishing any card returned from grave to hand,
able to detach to negate an activated DARK monster effect and destroy it.
This card was so clearly designed to be a catch-all to stop Shaddoll and Burning Abyss
in the meta that it's not even funny,
able to be easily made in Tellarknight from turn two onwards,
or any deck for that matter, as long as they could put up the three materials
needed to summon `Stellarknight Delteros`.
`Soul Transition` could tribute a level four normal summoned monster to draw two
if you control no special summoned monsters,
which really should have been labeled a Qli card
with how clearly targeted to the strategy it was.
Lastly, `Blaze Accelerator Reload` was a continuous trap
that could be considered `Tri-Blaze Accelerator` on field,
could dump a Volcanic from hand to draw one,
and could be banished from grave to send a Volcanic from deck to grave.
While all of this may seem niche and just a way to get the old GX archetype
some more playability,
Reload would actually move to spawn an entire new deck type to the meta with Volcanics,
which we'll cover with an upcoming YCS.

All in all, Secrets of Eternity was a mixed bag on release,
not having much impact in the short term,
but over the course of the year would have its pieces become more and more relevant
with the release of further cards.
This wouldn't be the only release, though, heading into the first YCS of the year,
as the year's first structure deck would also drop before then
to help shake up the format just a little more.

### HERO Strike Structure Deck

- Release date: January 30th, 2015
- Set type: structure deck
- Major strategies: HERO
- Impact: how do I get through this section without being biased?

The HERO Strike structure deck would once again usher in a return of HERO support
to the game, as is tradition at least once in an era.
This time would bring the introduction of a massive overhaul
to the Masked HERO sub-archetype introduced at the start of Zexal,
bringing a new supporting Elemental HERO in the form of `Elemental HERO Shadow Mist`,
able to search a Change quick-play spell on special summon and a HERO when sent to grave,
a couple of new Masked HERO support spells in `Form Change`,
which lets you tag a Masked HERO into a different attributed one,
`Mask Change II`, which allows you to Mask Change any monster at the cost of a discard,
and `Mask Charge`, to recur back a HERO and a Change quick-play,
and last but not least, new Masked HEROes in `Masked HERO Koga`,
a LIGHT target that boosts by 500 for each opponent's monster
and can banish a HERO from grave to drop an opponent's monster by its attack,
`Masked HERO Divine Wind`, a WIND target that is battle destruction immune,
locks the opponent to one monster attacking a turn
and draws on destroying something in battle,
and the big man himself, `Masked HERO Dark Law`,
a DARK target that puts a one-sided Macro effect on the opponent
and banishes a card from their hand if they add a card from deck to hand.

It should be no secret that these are my personal favorite cards in the history of the game,
so I'll try and be as unbiased as I possibly can.
On initial release, the Masked HERO pieces looked absolutely wonderful
in terms of taking HERO in a new direction that was potentially meta relevant,
namely on the back of Dark Law himself:
by special summoning Shadow Mist through a HERO, `Summoner Monk`,
or a more niche option like `Goblindbergh` or `Tin Goldfish`,
you could search for the Mask Change you need to convert her into Dark Law
the following turn, which would then net you a HERO search
while having immediate applications.
Masked HERO for now would not see much meta play,
though an interesting place would be found for the new HEROes almost immediately.
Because the newly released Mask Change II could change any monster into a Masked HERO,
there was no need to run all of the HERO cards,
as you could instead tech it in your side deck in any predominantly DARK strategy,
like Burning Abyss or Shaddoll, to make Dark Law out of any DARK body.
Was this super relevant at this point in time? Not really,
but it was a solid option playing into Burning Abyss specifically,
because the only in-archetype outs that they played were Fire Lake and Virgil,
as Dante couldn't boost itself under Dark Law,
which meant that they had lost substantial resources to out the Macro effect.

#### YCS Charleston

YCS Charleston would be held the same weekend, being a half sealed event,
and following the release of Secrets, a couple of things would shift,
but the overall vibe would remain the same.
With the additions of Farfa and Libic,
Burning Abyss would solidify its placement as the top deck of the format,
also taking first place, piloted by Jonathon Castillo Gomez to top 16,
who did not make his particular list public.
Qliphort adopted its new pieces in immediately,
seeing one Monolith and two to three Stealth added into topping lists,
alongside the new draw tool of Soul Transition,
being effectively Pot of Greed for Qliphort specifically.
Tellarknight would continue to see roughly tier 2 to 3 success rates,
with Diamond specifically giving them a better matchup into Burning Abyss,
but still being substantially behind the top deck.
Shaddoll had taken a serious fall from grace here,
being the only heavily affected deck of the previous ban list,
in addition to receiving no new relevant support from Secrets like the other strategies had,
leaving it in a position to adapt or die moving into the new format.

However, the list that turned the most heads unquestionably was Volcanic,
which shockingly went completely undefeated in the Swiss rounds.
Reload had turned the Volcanic pieces into an insane value engine,
discarding Shells for the draws and searching another right after,
`Volcanic Scattershot` being the dump-from-deck target
to both board nuke and burn the opponent for 1500 too,
and `Royal Firestorm Guards` being present to reload the engine for further shots.
`Magic Planter` would also see major success because of this,
able to send a Reload to grave to draw two,
then fire the Reload from grave to Scattershot away a board.
This particular strategy would be a rogue option for the next few months,
always popping up as a one-of in top cuts at most,
usually in tandem with another strategy for added power.

Regardless, Charleston had laid the groundwork for an interesting format to develop for sure,
but that was all about to be completely upended.
For the fifth time in the game's history, we were about to enter a tier zero format,
and judging by how certain aspects of the set played out,
Konami knew exactly what they were doing with this one.

### The Secret Forces

- Release date: February 13th, 2015
- Set type: deck building set
- Major strategies: Yosenju, Nekroz, Ritual Beast
- Impact: wait, you can make rituals not suck?

Well, this kind of pack was not explicitly called this at the time.
In reality, The Secret Forces would be the prototype
for what eventually became known as the deck building set,
a set that introduces three archetypes,
whether it be a new take on an older archetype or a completely original one,
and jams a whole ton of support to get the strategy on the ground running out the gate,
with a couple of additional pieces trickled out over the coming core sets.
As such, this set would introduce three new archetypes
we were actually introduced to back in Secrets,
with each being a unique take on a previous mechanic.

Yosenju would be the first and arguably the worst of the three,
a series of Beast-Warriors focused on chaining multiple normal summons together
and bouncing back to hand at the end of the turn.
This included the Kamas, each able to perform an additional Yosenju normal on normal summon,
with `Yosenju Kama 1` able to bounce a monster to hand if you controlled another Yosenju,
`Yosenju Kama 2` able to attack directly for half damage,
and `Yosenju Kama 3` able to search a Yosenju when a Yosenju deals battle damage,
all returning to hand in the end phase of normal summon.
They also received `Yosenju Tsujik` back in Secrets,
able to boost a Yosenju by a thousand for the turn,
or be used from the hand as a thousand boost battle trick,
also returning to hand the turn it's normaled.
While a decent theme, it entirely gets disjointed by the introduction of pendulums here too,
with the Shinchus providing scales 3 to 11
and `Mayosenju Daibak` being a level 10 that bounces two cards on normal or pendulum summon,
returning to the hand in the end phase of the turn it's specialed.
Lastly, `Yosen Training Grounds` is their archetypal gateway,
gaining a counter on each Yosenju summon and able to remove three to search a Yosenju card,
and `Yosenjus' Secret Move` is the archetypal omni negate counter trap,
only usable if you control only Yosenju.
While a fun strategy for the casual level,
Yosenju here suffered from a severe lack of focus,
as it seemed like the Kamas and the pendulums were at odds with each other
in terms of how the deck was actually supposed to be played,
being a pendulum spam strategy or a normal summon swarm strategy,
as if you pendulum summoned the Kamas, their return to hand effects would not trigger
and they'd be sitting ducks for the following turn,
leaving Yosenju as easily the least playable of the three strategies.

The Ritual Beasts were up next,
a series that actually was made of two different sub-archetypes,
being the Ritual Beast Tamers, a series of Psychics aimed at tutoring out another Ritual Beast,
with `Ritual Beast Tamer Lara` reviving a Ritual Beast on normal,
and `Ritual Beast Tamer Elder` giving you an extra normal summon
and, when returning, a banished Ritual Beast to field,
and the Spiritual Beasts, a series of animal companions,
including `Spiritual Beast Apelio`, who can banish an RB to boost all RBs by 500 that turn,
`Spiritual Beast Pettlephin`, who banishes an RB in hand to bounce a card,
`Spiritual Beast Cannahawk`, who banishes an RB from deck to grave,
and `Spiritual Beast Rampengu` from Secrets,
who banishes an RB from extra deck to dump a same typed RB from deck to grave.
The archetype's primary gimmick was contact fusion,
able to make any of their fusions by banishing a Tamer and a Spiritual Beast,
with all sharing an effect to return to the extra deck
to summon a banished Tamer and Spiritual Beast on quick effect,
with `Ritual Beast Ulti-Apelio` being immune to other cards for its attacks,
`Ritual Beast Ulti-Pettlephin` unable to die by card effect,
and `Ritual Beast Ulti-Cannahawk` able to return two banished RBs to grave
to search an RB card.
All the RB main deck monsters also have the stipulation
of being special summonable once per turn,
so while you could theoretically tag between your fusions and them over and over again,
in practice there was a limit to how many times you could do it.
As for their spell traps, `Ritual Beast's Bond` lets you contact fuse on a quick-play spell
using any two RBs rather than needing specific types,
`Ritual Beast Steeds` lets you non-target pop cards up to the number of RBs you control,
and `Ritual Beast Ambush` from Secrets
lets you summon back a graved or banished Tamer and Spiritual Beast.

A fun little fact about Ulti-Cannahawk's effect specifically:
because of wording, his search effect does not have to return both targets to grave
to get the search off, it just needs to return at least one,
which means you can actually chain its tag out effect to the search
to resummon one of the targets and preserve your resources.
Incidentally, did you also know that Ulti-Cannahawk is not a hard once per turn?
Ritual Beasts would absolutely abuse this interaction as much as they possibly could,
to chain together searches in excess for more pieces to continue search chains,
as well as interaction points in Steeds and setup for future plays in Bond and Ambush.
And because of the sheer versatility of this, Ritual Beast would actually be a contender
in the upcoming metagame,
mostly off the back of Steeds' non-targeting pop being extremely useful
for one specific matchup,
which would lead the deck into a tier three position for the coming months.

Lastly, and undoubtedly the most important, Nekroz was a series of ritual monsters
aimed at being the most generically accessible take on rituals so far,
with each of the ritual monsters having some effect in hand requiring them to be discarded,
and another on the field, usually aimed at interacting with extra deck summoned monsters.
`Nekroz of Clausolas` discards itself to search a Nekroz spell trap
and can drop an extra deck summoned monster to zero and negate its effects on a quick effect.
`Nekroz of Brionac` discards itself to search a Nekroz monster
and can bounce up to two extra deck summoned monsters once per turn.
`Nekroz of Trishula` can discard itself to negate anything that targets a Nekroz,
and banish a card from hand, field and grave on summon.
Yes, you have to hit one of each or none at all.
`Nekroz of Unicore` can discard itself to recur a Nekroz from grave to hand,
and is a Skill Drain for extra deck summoned monsters.
`Nekroz of Valkyrus` can discard itself and banish a Nekroz in grave
to negate an attack and end the battle phase,
and can tribute up to two monsters on field or in hand to draw that many cards.
And `Nekroz of Gungnir` from Secrets can discard itself
to make a Nekroz immune to destruction that turn,
and can discard a Nekroz to pop a card on field.
As for their ritual spells, each has an effect to banish itself and a Nekroz monster
from grave while you control no monsters to search another Nekroz spell,
with each also having its own unique gimmick:
`Nekroz Mirror` able to banish Nekroz tributes from grave for the summon,
`Nekroz Kaleidoscope` able to use extra deck monsters for the tributes,
also able to summon out multiple Nekroz off of one monster tributed,
and `Nekroz Cycle` from Secrets able to ritual summon a Nekroz that's in the graveyard.
Lastly would be the singular non-ritual Nekroz that was important at this stage,
`Shurit, Strategist of the Nekroz`,
able to consider itself the whole tribute for a Nekroz ritual summon
and searches a Warrior Nekroz ritual monster when tributed.

"It's these Don Act."
So in case it wasn't clear,
Nekroz was one of the most consistent decks to ever exist in the game's history up until this point,
thanks to years of ritual support waiting in the backlog,
like `Manju of the Ten Thousand Hands`, `Preparation of Rites`,
and even the recent `Herald of the Arc Light` for usage with Kaleidoscope.
You had easy access to literally every single card in the archetype,
thanks to Brionac, who could search any monster in the archetype,
which included Clausolas, who could search the spells.
While their power level would be seemingly lacking due to mostly being reactive effects,
this was all just on paper,
as regularly a Nekroz player could set up their resources on the first turn
to block you from advancing even slightly
and completely run you out of the game after with proper resource management.

The player base knew Nekroz was going to be tier zero immediately on release,
and seemingly Konami did too,
as the most important piece of the deck, Brionac,
was seemingly a lot harder to pull than any of the other cards.
This particular pack was structured as a five card pack,
with four super rares and one secret rare in every pack,
which naturally leads one to believe that the 20 secret rares
would each have a similar chance to be pulled in that spot,
but that was not the case.
Brionac, Trishula and Valkyrus were short printed very heavily,
with all three seemingly being pulled at a rate of roughly one per every six boxes,
which, while not nearly as bad as `Crush Card Virus` back in the original Gold Series,
was still absolutely ludicrous for a deck everyone knew was going to be tier 1,
if not tier zero.
As such, prices skyrocketed, with Brionac at its peak pulling a price tag of around $150,
with Valkyrus and Trishula also being extremely pricey,
but not quite on the same level as Brionac,
and the results from the next YCS would do nothing to help that price point.

#### YCS Tacoma

YCS Tacoma would be the first event held after The Secret Forces released, on February 15th,
and though it was a half sealed event,
Nekroz would prove to be an absolutely dominant strategy right out the gate.
The consistency of the deck would prove to be absolutely incredible,
able to handle just about any scenario thrown at it,
but there was one more specific interaction Nekroz had
that would prove to be insanely problematic.
Released all the way back in 2009, `Djinn Releaser of Rituals`
could be used from grave as ritual cost,
and gave any monster summoned using it a one-sided special summon floodgate
for as long as it was on the field.
Up until now, it was believed that ritual was too slow a mechanic to ever use it reliably,
but with the release of Nekroz,
they would utilize the level four normal summon searchers of Manju
and `Senju of the Thousand Hands`, to use alongside Nekroz of Unicore,
to access `Lavalval Chain`, which in turn would dump the Djinn from deck
to use as material for the summon of a monster like Clausolas,
which, as it turned out, would be incredibly easy to pull off reliably
given how consistent the deck was.
The Djinn lock, as it became known, would be the bane of the format,
with a common consensus being that a one-sided lock sucked all skill out of the format,
which would also lead to one of the most controversial angle shoots in the game's history,
which we'll cover soon.

Qliphort would actually move to do fairly well here,
thanks to being one of the only decks that didn't auto lose to the Djinn lock,
as normal summoning a Qli with `Saqlifice` equipped
was shockingly usually enough to get out of the lock,
providing a slower path to a boss on it,
or by ensuring that you could use the tribute effect of Carrier.
That didn't mean it was always going to work:
there were extremely simple counters to this line,
like MST and Trishula's targeting negation,
but it was usually consistent enough to get the job done.
Burning Abyss was the only other deck to make top cut here,
and that was primarily thanks in large part to Farfa,
as by temporarily banishing the Djinn monster from the board,
a Burning Abyss player could continue their lines as normal,
which was still countered by Trishula's negation effect.
Barrett Keys would win the event in the sealed draft, making it to top 16 with Nekroz,
marking the deck's technically first YCS win,
leading into the year's Gold Series under a week later.

### Premium Gold: Return of the Bling

- Release date: March 20th, 2015 (the video says February 20th)
- Set type: import reprint set
- Major strategies: Blackwing, Masked HERO, Galaxy-Eyes
- Impact: a theorized meta breaker

Return of the Bling would be the second crack at turning the yearly Gold Series sets
into an import source for the TCG to receive some cards not yet brought in from the OCG,
and for the most part, as usual, these imports were not all that impressive,
but would hold some niche applications,
such as `Absolute King Back Jack`, a new wave of Blackwing support, `Cat Shark`,
`Masked HERO Anki`, "lover" and `Santa Claws`.
However, there was one card among these that held legitimate potential.
`Number 95: Galaxy-Eyes Dark Matter Dragon` could be overlaid onto any Galaxy-Eyes Xyz,
and could dump three Dragons from deck on summon
to make the opponent banish three monsters from deck,
able to attack twice in the battle phase by detaching material.
With the currently limited Dragon Rulers,
Dark Matter was instantly theorized as the perfect way to set up your board
for large scale pushes, being hailed as the return of Dragon Rulers to the format,
but we'd have to wait and see if it could actually convert into results.

In addition to these, a series of currently banned cards
would see significant erratas to their effects,
in order to potentially return to the game on the next ban list.
`Sinister Serpent` received a clause in its effect
to banish a Sinister Serpent in grave during the opponent's next end phase,
which effectively meant that if you discarded it within a turn of bringing it back,
it was gone for good.
`Temple of the Kings` would see its trap condition limited to one trap a turn rather than all,
heavily hampering its FTK potential.
`Exchange of the Spirit` would now require both players to have 15 plus cards in grave
to activate, rather than just the controller, killing its FTK potential.
`Ring of Destruction` now had to be used on the opponent's turn,
could only target their monsters,
and can't target something that would burn them for game on activation,
and on top of all of that, you took the burn damage first to ensure no tie games,
placing so many new restrictions on the card that it was almost not worth it anymore.
Lastly, and most importantly for the short term,
Crush Card Virus lost its three turn effect,
blocked the opponent from taking damage for the rest of the turn,
and let them destroy up to three monsters that met the condition from their deck.
While this was considerably worse than the original version by a country mile,
this would actually be theoretically good for certain decks in certain metagames,
but we'd have to see how it plays out the following ban list.

#### YCS Prague

YCS Prague would be held two weeks later, on March 1st,
and once again Nekroz would show its dominance on the top cut,
but this time would not take the event overall.
Valens Leteurtre would hold that honor, taking the event with Burning Abyss,
sporting three main deck copies of Mask Change II to access Dark Law,
in a metagame where his presence was becoming more and more useful by the day,
as a one-sided Macro and search banisher was shockingly competent into Nekroz.

#### YCS Guatemala

YCS Guatemala would be a week later,
though unfortunately we know next to nothing about this particular event,
outside of Erick Villanueva Priego taking the event after top cut sealed draft.

#### ARG Circuit Series Fort Lauderdale

But another notable event in the game's history would be the same weekend.
The ARG Circuit Series Fort Lauderdale would be held the same weekend as Guatemala,
and though we haven't discussed ARG's tournaments up until this point,
this particular event is extremely important to both the history of the game itself
and the community at large, due to a specific choice made by a top player.
For those unaware, ARG, or Alter Reality Games, was a shop at the time
that would regularly hold their own major tournaments,
completely separate from Konami's oversight.
As such, these tournaments are an interesting topic for covering at another time,
but this particular event would have Patrick Hoban, a major player of the era,
running both a Djinn in the main deck and a Djinn in the sideboard of his Nekroz deck.
Because of the belief that Djinn sucked the skill out of a match,
a common practice at the time was to, after game one,
ask the opponent if they would like to gentleman's out a Djinn in the main,
with both players agreeing to move Djinn to the side decks for games two and three.
Hoban developed an angle shoot that he used for this specific event:
gentleman's-ing out the Djinn in the main
to bring in a second copy from his side deck,
to effectively trick the opponent into playing a suboptimal deck.
When this news broke on the specific usage,
the debate was immediately sparked on whether or not this action was both legal and ethical.
While technically legal, as Konami would update their rules document in 2019
to specifically bar this kind of action from ever happening again,
the ethics of it were highly suspect,
to the point where Hoban ended up writing an entire article on the issue for ARG's website,
which has since been archived.
I will include a link to the article in the description below
and let you draw your own conclusions on the incident itself,
but it would be doing a disservice to the history of the game to at least not mention it.
Regardless of the ethics,
this would be the biggest case of the gentleman's out trend that had been present for years,
with the first real case being `Return from the Different Dimension`, and Six Samurai,
and Dragon Rulers,
and with the next ban list, it wouldn't be the last case of gentleman's agreements
rearing its head in Yu-Gi-Oh.

#### Ban list (April 1st)

The new ban list would take effect on April 1st,
and it would bring a massive wave of changes to the meta at large.
Newly banned were the four Dragon Rulers, nipping any chance of Dark Matter
making an impact in the bud,
and Snatch Steal, in one of the funniest "oops, we messed up" moments
in the history of the ban list.
Newly limited were `Tour Guide From the Underworld` as a hit to BA,
Preparation of Rites as a hit to Nekroz,
Saqlifice and `Skill Drain` as hits to Qliphort,
`Symbol of Heritage` as a hit to an FTK that could pop up with an unlimit later,
`Vanity's Emptiness` as a hit to the staple floodgate,
`Dragon Ravine` returning from the banning of the Rulers,
and Sinister Serpent, Temple of the Kings, Crush Card Virus, Exchange of the Spirit
and Ring of Destruction, all following their erratas.
Newly semi-limited were Nekroz of Brionac as a hit to Nekroz,
`Qliphort Scout` as a hit to Qli,
and `Legendary Six Samurai - Shi En`, `Charge of the Light Brigade`
and `Sacred Sword of Seven Stars`, all up from one.
Lastly, newly unlimited were `Brotherhood of the Fire Fist - Spirit`, the baby Rulers,
`Gladiator Beast Bestiari`, `Gorz the Emissary of Darkness`, Goyo, `Lonefire Blossom`
and `Hieratic Seal of Convocation`, being a mass cleanup wave.

Needless to say, this ban list moved a lot of cards up and off the list,
but didn't make too many moves that would impact the actual meta.
Nekroz effectively lost three copies of Brionac between its hit and the Prep hit,
but it was still without question the most powerful deck in the game.
The Qli hit was far more potent,
as losing two copies of Saqlifice heavily hampered the deck's Nekroz matchup.
Lastly, the hit towards Burning Abyss's one card combo of Tour Guide was impactful,
but not detrimental to the deck that is entirely made of extenders.

#### Ultimaya Tzolkin promo

In addition to the ban list, a new Shonen Jump promo would drop in `Ultimaya Tzolkin`,
who can be summoned by sending two high level monsters with the same level,
a tuner and a non-tuner, to grave,
and cheats out level seven or eight Dragon Synchros when you set a card,
and can't be attacked or targeted while there's another Synchro on the board.
While interesting on paper, Tzolkin's main issue at this stage was a lack of good targets
to summon, as the only decent targets were Synchros from the 5D's era,
causing it to not see play for now, but that could easily change with a better target.

#### YCS Chicago

YCS Chicago would be held two weeks later, and to no one's surprise,
Nekroz remained the top deck, seeing over half of the top cut
and being the deck the winner, Steven De Lara, piloted into the top 16 draft.
The slap on the wrist that they received on the previous ban list
had done jack all to the deck's insane consistency,
and the Djinn lock still being present was a headache for most to deal with.
Shaddoll and Tellarknight would both see a surprising resurgence, however,
with unfortunately no public list for Tellarknights being available to analyze here.
Shaddoll, on the other hand, had received a boon on the previous ban list
in the form of Crush Card Virus,
as both it and Burning Abyss could use the new tool in the current metagame
in a surprisingly effective manner.
As Nekroz was the dominant deck of the format,
Crush Card actually did a shocking number on the strategy,
able to rip most of their rituals out of hand,
and giving no real way to benefit from the deck destruction downside added on,
allowing Shaddoll and Burning Abyss to both keep a foot in the door for the format.
Qliphort had to adapt with the ban list hit to Saqlifice,
with the most common pivot being to the trap Re-qliate,
able to boost your Qliphorts back to full on normal,
while also stunning low level normal summons and high level special summons.
Ritual Beast would see its first premier event top,
showcasing its resource loop quite effectively
and having an actually surprisingly good Nekroz matchup,
thanks to Steeds being able to non-target pop a Djinn lock.
This would leave the format a bit too similar to before the ban list,
but with some clear changes in the air,
and those changes would only be pushed forward by the OCG import set
that followed two days later.

### World Superstars

- Release date: April 17th, 2015 (the video says April 14th)
- Set type: import set
- Major strategies: Heroic, Star Seraph, Galaxy
- Impact: "knock with my world"

World Superstars would bring a handful of cards we've been missing out on in the TCG
to the game, and while some were clearly too late to have their impact felt,
particularly in Nekroz, there were still a couple of imports here worth discussing.
`Galaxy Soldier` could special summon itself by sending a LIGHT from hand to grave,
then search another Galaxy monster.
Soldier specifically would be considered for LIGHT decks
because of its ability to access `Constellar Pleiades`,
as by summoning it, you could search another to immediately summon for the rank five access.
This would be experimented with for the time,
but the only deck that could consistently do anything with it would be Tellarknight,
which was not completely sold on its inclusion.

The Heroic archetype would also see its first support since the Zexal era here.
`Heroic Challenger - Assault Halberd` could special summon itself
if your opponent has a monster and you don't,
and pierces, searching a Heroic on damage.
`Heroic Challenger - Thousand Blades` can discard a Heroic to summon a Heroic from deck,
locking to Heroics, and can summon itself from grave when you take damage.
And `Number 86: Heroic Champion - Rhongomyniad` was a Warrior-locked rank four
that could be made with up to five materials, gaining effects for each,
which in order includes battle destruction immunity, a 1500 boost,
card effect immunity, summon locking and a board nuke.
While extremely impressive, Rhongomyniad had no real enablers at the time,
outside of specifically Tellarknight once again,
and even then the fourth material is really what mattered in its case,
unable to reliably reach that going first there,
causing it to see no real play,
although Halberd and Blades would both see experimentation over the coming months
with upcoming releases that would allow them to stick around longer in the short term.

`Star Seraph Scepter` can search a Star Seraph on summon,
and if used for an Xyz of three plus materials, can pop a card on field to draw one.
`Star Seraph Sovereignty` can, when a Star Seraph is summoned,
special summon itself from hand and draw one,
only able to be used for an Xyz that takes three plus materials.
Scepter and Sovereignty, or stick and chair as they became known in the community,
were extremely interesting options for decks that wanted access to Delteros or Diamond,
as opening one of each could get you there,
thanks to Scepter's search and Sovereignty's summon going onto a chain with each other
from Scepter's summon,
meaning that by searching another Sovereignty,
you could trigger its summon effect off the summon of the first Sovereignty,
due to how hand knowledge worked,
resulting in anywhere from two to three draws and two pops from these cards alone,
which would see splash play as an engine for the near future.

Lastly for the set, `Number F0: Utopic Future` could be made
using two non-Number Xyz of the same rank,
can't be destroyed by battle, reflects damage back to the opponent,
and takes control of anything it battles for the battle phase,
able to protect itself from destruction by detaching material.
This would be slotted into BA thanks to its ability to convert used up rank 3s
into a body that does something,
as well as being a Dante holder, as Dante would still trigger when detached off,
similar to how `Downerd Magician` had been used.

#### YCS Bogota

YCS Bogota would be held the same weekend,
and would be the end of an era in its own way,
as this would be the final event to do sealed draft top cuts in the history of the game,
as of the writing of the script.
From these results, though the meta had remained mostly the same,
there were a couple of adaptations worth looking into.
Shaddoll would be the primary strategy seeing a change in its game plan,
as with the rising need for more relevant LIGHTs in its lineup for access to Construct,
the new stick and chair filled that niche optimally,
giving the deck access to Evilswarm or `Evilswarm Ouroboros` of all things
to provide a hand rip going first, in addition to the draw two off chair.
This would become the norm for Shaddolls moving forward,
including stick and chair into lists for the added draw power
and the three material rank four access.
Barrett Keys would take the event, piloting to top 16 with Nekroz,
who did not make his list public.

#### YCS Bochum

YCS Bochum would be held two weeks later,
and with the discovery of stick and chair for the deck,
Shaddoll rose quite substantially in representation,
but would not be the most interesting happenings of this particular event.
Mecha Phantom Beast had cleared its way into top cut for the first time ever as its own deck,
utilizing the newly released `Mecha Phantom Beast Jaculuslan` from World Superstars
to rip an opponent's hand apart on the first turn,
able to make it so far primarily off the surprise factor of it.
Volcanic HERO would also be a new take on the rogue level strategy,
utilizing Summoner Monk to access Shadow Mist for the Dark Law lines,
while also utilizing the standard Volcanic package
that had carried the strategy into the fringes of the meta.
Luca Chetoni would take the event on Nekroz,
notably playing a copy of `D.D. Warrior Lady` in the main
as an option to remove the Djinn lock,
that was searchable with a semi-limited ROTA.
This would lead into the next core set,
and though Secrets before it was not too impactful on the meta at large,
this one would introduce a series of monsters
that would change the perception of the game forever, with the right amount of time.

### Crossed Souls

- Release date: May 15th, 2015
- Set type: core set
- Major strategies: Frightfur, Zefra, Infernoid
- Impact: a ghost girl that would shape the future

Crossed Souls was the second core set of the year,
and as far as core sets go, while not ushering in a new era of the game,
it did bring a couple of pieces that would shape a couple of movements in the meta
over the coming years.

Starting with the new archetype, Zefra is a series of pendulum monsters
that take a bunch of the dual-terminal lore archetypes of recent releases
and give them a couple of new support pieces,
tied to the idea of a centralized archetype.
In reality, this archetype would do next to nothing as Zefra,
but would give a couple of their tie-in archetypes new tools to tinker with.
Yang Zing would see `Zefraxi, Treasure of the Yang Zing`,
who makes a Yang Zing a tuner when summoned from the deck
and goes to the bottom of deck when it leaves field,
and `Zefraniu, Secret of the Yang Zing`,
who searches a Yang Zing spell trap when summoned from deck.
These two would do a number of things for Yang Zing,
by giving them more varied Synchro access,
as well as, and far more importantly, the ability to search for `Yang Zing Creation`
and `Yang Zing Path`, boosting the deck's consistency considerably,
to where Yang Zing could be considered on the fringes of rogue now,
but still not properly meta.
Ritual Beast would get `Ritual Beast Tamer Zeframpilica`,
who summons an RB from grave on normal, popping it in the end phase,
and `Ritual Beast Tamer Zefrawendi`,
who adds a Zefra from face-up extra deck to hand on normal.
Obviously one of these was way better than the other here,
with Zeframpilica seeing play in RB moving forward as a mid to late game push.
Lastly, `Oracle of Zefra` searches a Zefra on activation,
and gives effects when you summon a Zefra or a monster using Zefra as material
from the extra deck or ritual summons.
This would mostly be disregarded, as Zefra wasn't the best archetype,
but an interesting condition of the card is that if you cannot perform the search,
you cannot activate it,
which, while irrelevant now, would see an interesting application in a few years.

Fluffal would get some of its first decent support in `Edge Imp Chain`,
who searches a Frightfur card on being sent to grave
and searches another Chain when it attacks,
`Frightfur Sheep`, who takes Chain and a Fluffal,
stuns effects when attacking and reborns itself on destruction, boosting by 800,
and `Frightfur Fusion`, a Miracle Fusion for Frightfurs.
While still not close to meta,
Fluffal support was finally heading in the right direction,
which would see some experimentation later this year with more releases.

Infernoid would see three new monsters in `Infernoid Pirmais`,
who spins back a card without letting that card respond once per turn,
`Infernoid Sjette`, who makes an opponent banish a monster from their extra deck on attack,
and `Infernoid Devyaty`, who nukes all spells and traps on summon except Void cards,
and can tribute a monster to negate and banish a monster using its effect.
Devyaty was obviously the most talked about piece here,
having a similar impact to Onuncu
without the downside of removing her tribute fodder with her nuke effect,
which in turn would cause Infernoid's playability to shoot up.

Burning Abyss would see new pieces in `Draghig, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss`,
who stacks a BA card from deck,
and `Barbar, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss`,
who can banish up to three BAs in grave to burn for 300 for each.
Draghig and Barbar would both see their niches in the current version of BA,
with Barbar in particular filling a gap in the deck's ability to close out games over time.

U.A. would see decent support this round, with `U.A. Signing Deal`,
able to cheat out a U.A. from deck by burning the user for its level times 300,
and negates its effects and locks it from being Synchro or Xyz material,
and `U.A. Penalty Box`, making U.A.s able to temporarily banish anything they attack,
and can be banished from grave to search a U.A. spell.
These two would both be solid for the strategy,
with Signing Deal being a starter the deck desperately needed
and Penalty Box giving the deck spell searching,
but without a way to reliably get it in grave,
U.A. would continue to see zero play in the meta.

On to the one-offs.
`Performapal Lizardraw` would be another option for the theoretical pendulum pile,
able to send itself from scale to extra if you have another Performapal in scale
to draw one, which, with its high scale and typing, made it a consideration.
`El Shaddoll Anoyatyllis` would be the WATER Shaddoll fusion,
blocking special summons from hand or grave using spell traps,
which couldn't be more targeted at Nekroz if it actively tried to be,
giving Shaddoll an in-archetype counter.
`Ritual Beast Ulti-Gaiapelio` was a new RB contact fusion,
taking a Spiritual Beast, a Tamer and an RB fusion,
able to banish an RB in hand to negate anything that activates.
This was undoubtedly a formidable end boss,
but the lack of a tag out effect and requiring a significant amount of resource burning
to pull off left it in a weird space, though it would see play.
`Tellarknight Ptolemaeus` takes two or more level fours,
can detach three to rank up into a LIGHT rank five,
and can detach seven to skip the opponent's next turn,
able to attach a Tellarknight from extra deck to it in the end phase.
Those last two effects, I don't think anyone has ever actually read,
as Ptolemaeus had two uses from the word go:
either to be used to rank up into Pleiades using three materials,
or to access Diamond in any strategy using just two.
As such, it would see play immediately and hold its niches for a certain matchup,
but would become far more of a headache within the next year for other reasons.
`Galaxy Cyclone` pops a set spell trap on activation
and can be banished from grave to pop a face-up one,
being a double up on spell trap removal at the cost of being more difficult to use,
which would see some experimentation.
`Unexpected Dai` special summons a normal level four or lower from your deck
if you control no monsters,
being talked about for Synchro and Xyz toolboxes,
but being discarded almost immediately,
as there was no real benefit to running it and a regular vanilla in your deck.
`Lose 1 Turn` negates monster effects the turn they're special summoned
and swaps them to defense mode on summon,
being so clearly targeted towards using Qli that it was almost laughable,
seeing play immediately.
`Moon Mirror Shield` makes its equipped monster boost to the attack value
of whatever it battles plus 100,
cycling itself to top or bottom of deck on destruction for 500 life points.
This was a stun player's dream,
as it made the most problematic but weak monsters in the game
impossible to out through battle, seeing play for that reason alone,
but not being as powerful as you might remember in the meta at large,
due to various answers to it existing in the format,
as well as it potentially locking its user out of the game
if its recursion is used incorrectly.
`The Melody of Awakening Dragon`, for the cost of a discard,
searches two Dragons with 3,000 or more attack and 2500 or less defense,
which reads as Blue-Eyes pretty much any way you slice it,
which, while not useful now, would see considerable play in the coming year.
`Galaxy-Eyes Full Armor Photon Dragon` takes three level eights or another Galaxy-Eyes Xyz,
can suck up equips as materials, and can detach one to pop a face-up card once per turn,
seeing play as a tool for rank eight strategies.
Lastly, `Ghost Ogre & Snow Rabbit` could be discarded
to pop any card that uses an effect after it's already face-up,
specifically being useful in countering out continuous cards and field spells.
While it seemed decent and fairly unassuming on the surface,
Ghost Ogre would be the start of a new era in hand traps,
which would over the following years explode in their utility
as more and more ghost girls join the fray.

#### YCS Columbus

YCS Columbus would be the testing grounds for these new tools,
as well as the 150th YCS, held a week after Crossed Souls released,
and no changes had been seen in the meta at large,
outside of BA including its new monsters
and rank four accessible decks using Ptolemaeus.
Terrance Joseph Kinsley would take the event on Nekroz,
notably playing a copy of `Buster Blader` and copies of `Book of Eclipse`
as a way to answer the Djinn lock,
which would become an option many players would use moving forward.

#### Oceania WCQ

This would also mark the beginning of the WCQ season,
with the Oceania WCQ being held two weeks later,
and similar to Columbus, the format held unchanged.
Oliver Parle would take the event on Nekroz,
similarly playing lockouts in Warrior Lady and Eclipse.

#### Central America WCQ

The Central America WCQ would be two weeks later,
and though the meta was still fairly consistent to what we had seen up until this point,
a new face would crack into top 32 here.
Infernoid would finally see a premier event top,
utilizing a small Lightsworn engine to facilitate both early mills
and to set up Infernoid summons,
as well as to access utility like `Lyla, Lightsworn Sorceress`'s spell trap popping
and `Ehren, Lightsworn Monk`'s ability to out anything flipped by Book of Eclipse.
Galileo De Obaldia Soza would take the event on Nekroz,
being mostly standard, but notably including three copies of `Solemn Scolding`
in the backrow, thanks to Nekroz's next to no trap lineup allowing the utility.

#### North American WCQ

The North American WCQ was next, held a week later,
and once again it's the same story as usual,
with the only outlier being a single HERO list that squeaked into top 64,
utilizing the newly released Assault Halberd as a rank four extension piece,
as well as Goblindbergh to special summon Shadow Mist for the search.
Noah Greene would take the event on Burning Abyss,
utilizing both the newly released Crush Card Virus errata,
as well as cards like `Karma Cut`, `Phoenix Wing Wind Blast` and `Raigeki Break`
to trigger BA effects on the opponent's turn.

#### Frightfur Tiger promo

As we cross into July for the year,
a new Shonen Jump promo would begin its distribution, being `Frightfur Tiger`,
a fusion of Sabres and at least one Fluffal monster,
able to pop cards on field on summon up to the materials used,
boosting Frightfurs by 300 for each Fluffal or Frightfur you control,
and there only ever being able to be one on field at a time.
This was absolutely the best fusion they had received so far,
giving Fluffals an actual boss monster worth summoning
that took the same materials as `Frightfur Wolf`,
meaning that you could set up for an OTK using the two in combination,
seeing experimentation at a rogue level with this release.

#### European WCQ

After that detour, the European WCQ was a week after NA,
and once again no real surprises, seeing a similar spread to the NA WCQ.
Ido Marcus would take the event on Shaddoll,
being the strongest performance the deck had had the entire year,
utilizing the monster `Peropero Cerperus` to access `El Shaddoll Shekhinaga`
and give removal afterwards, also dumpable with `Mathematician`.

#### South American WCQ

Lastly for the WCQ season, the South American WCQ would be held the same day,
and, stop me if you've heard this one before,
we'd see the same spread of decks in the top cut,
with Gerardo Delgado taking the event on Nekroz,
utilizing the Buster Blader option as well as the Solemn Scolding package.

#### Ban list (July 16th)

With the WCQ season done and in the books, a new ban list would take effect on July 16th,
and this would actually mark a significant change in how the ban list was updated,
as now it would no longer be standardly the first of the month,
like it had almost always been before.
This meant that the ban lists now had a little more unpredictability to them,
which would only grow in the coming years.
Newly banned were Djinn Releaser of Rituals and Lavalval Chain,
putting an official end to the Djinn lock, which by this point players were absolutely sick of.
Newly limited were Shurit, being one of the best options for Nekroz thanks to its search,
and `Trishula, Dragon of the Ice Barrier`, returning from the banned section.
Newly semi-limited were `Atlantean Dragoons` and Dragon Ravine, both returning from one,
and newly unlimited were Dark Strike Fighter, `Glow-Up Bulb`, Sinister Serpent,
Sacred Sword of Seven Stars, Temple of the Kings and Exchange of the Spirit,
finishing up the previous list's cleanup wave.
With the only hits being targeted at exclusively Nekroz,
Konami was making it clear that they knew the issues with the deck
prior to the WCQ season, and were now taking steps to correct it,
which would bring the deck more in line with other strategies of the era,
but by no means take it out of tier one contention.
This would lead into the next set, releasing the next day,
and with its previous iteration bringing meta staples to the game,
many expected something similar to happen this time.

### Dragons of Legend 2

- Release date: July 17th, 2015
- Set type: import set
- Major strategies: Critias and Hermos, Toon, Prediction Princess
- Impact: not spoken (only a laugh in the captions)

But Dragons of Legend 2 had massive shoes to fill,
as the last set brought the Hands and `Soul Charge`, which fundamentally changed the meta.
So what did this edition bring?
Unfortunately, this edition brought nothing of actual relevance to the game,
including a new block of support for Red-Eyes,
as well as new strategies in Critias, Hermos and Aquaactress,
all of which did jack in the meta.
Only two strategies here were worth talking about,
and that would be Prediction Princess and Toons.

So let's start with Prediction Princess.
`Prediction Princess Tarotrei` would be their boss monster,
a ritual that could special summon a Flip monster from grave in your end phase face-down,
and can flip a monster up or down on a quick effect once per turn.
This would be considered interesting,
but overall lacked anything worth flipping for the time being,
being relegated to the bulk pile for now.
Toon would be the strategy that brought in the most attention,
namely thanks to its new field spell `Toon Kingdom`,
which effectively was `Toon World` with targeting protection
and battle destruction prevention for your Toons,
giving the strategy more actual sticking power,
outside of the common threat of an MST to the book.
They also received `Comic Hand`, being an in-strategy version of Snatch Steal,
making its target a Toon and able to attack directly,
and `Mimicat`, able to steal anything from the opponent's grave to use for yourself.
While interesting for sure, Toon still saw no relevant play from this,
due to still having the same problem of the entire deck dying to a copy of MST,
which Kingdom did not solve.
This left the game with no real shakeup,
leaving any new developments to the next core set, releasing a month later.

### Clash of Rebellions

- Release date: August 7th, 2015
- Set type: core set
- Major strategies: Performage, Igknight, Kozmo
- Impact: the core set to end all core sets

I'm probably going to get a bit of flak on this one,
but I truly do believe that CORE should be regarded as one of the greatest core sets
of all time.
This singular set would introduce new engines, power cards and archetypes
that would completely define the course of the next year of Yu-Gi-Oh,
and sadly I believe it gets lost in the shuffle a little bit,
due to it coming between Duelist Alliance,
which is regularly referred to as the set that completely changed Yu-Gi-Oh, which is valid,
and a set in early 2017 that once again changed the game forever, which we'll get to.
Regardless, this is one of those sets that introduced so much
that would shape the game as we know it,
that we're going to need to speedrun through the less notable stuff first,
so that we can give those parts the time that they deserve.

Let's start with Red-Eyes getting a complementary wave of support
that syncs up with their Dragons of Legend 2 wave from the last month
by having next to nothing in common with it. Yay.
`Red-Eyes Black Flare Dragon` is a Gemini that burns the opponent for its attack
at the end of the battle phase it battles.
`The Black Stone of Legend` can tribute itself to summon a level seven or lower Red-Eyes
from deck, and it can recur itself from grave to hand
by shuffling back a Red-Eyes in grave.
`Black Metal Dragon` equips itself from hand onto a Red-Eyes and boosts it by 600,
and searches a Red-Eyes when sent from field to grave.
`Red-Eyes Archfiend of Lightning` is a Gemini
that can nuke anything with defense lower than its attack.
`Archfiend Black Skull Dragon` is a fusion of an Archfiend and a Red-Eyes normal
that stuns effects while attacking and burns at the end of the battle phase it attacks
for the attack of a Red-Eyes in grave, shuffling it back into deck.
`Red-Eyes Flare Metal Dragon` is a rank seven that has effect destruction immunity,
burns the opponent for 500 for using any card or effect,
and can detach to revive a normal Red-Eyes.
`Cards of the Red Stone` pitches a Red-Eyes from hand to draw two
and dump one from deck to grave.
And `Red-Eyes Fusion` lets you fusion summon a monster that uses Red-Eyes as material
using monsters in deck, but locks you out of other normal and special summons that turn.
Long and short, Red-Eyes as an archetype did nothing of note,
but Flare Metal would see play as a generic seven burn option,
and Fusion would be the first of three spells in the set that could fuse using materials
in the deck,
with all three of these finding meta relevance at one point in the game's history,
due to that effect being universally considered one of the best fusion effects possible,
though Red-Eyes Fusion's relevance wouldn't be for a handful of years.

Igknight was a series of normal pendulum monsters
that all share the effect to pop both scaled Igknights
to search a FIRE Warrior monster from deck or grave,
with two level threes in `Igknight Squire` and `Igknight Crusader`,
two level fours in `Igknight Templar` and `Igknight Paladin`,
a level five in `Igknight Margrave` and a level six in `Igknight Gallant`,
as well as a field spell in `Ignition Phoenix`,
which boosts all Igknights by 300
and lets you pop an Igknight card to search an Igknight card once per turn.
Igknight had some real applications in the rogue space,
thanks to being able to effectively pop your monsters to search scales,
then set scales and bring them all back for Xyz material,
but this came with the downside of, once you make an Xyz with them,
they're mostly gone for good by ending up in the grave,
which led to it being mostly unpopular.
However, there was one other piece that was tangentially related
that would iron out that obvious flaw.
`Luster Pendulum, the Dracoslayer` was a scale 5 level four tuner
that could only be used for the summon of Dracoslayer monsters,
and could pop your other scale to search another copy of that card once per turn
while in the scale.
This enabled Igknights to access `Ignister Prominence, the Blasting Dracoslayer`,
a level eight pendulum-locked Synchro that could summon a Dracoslayer from deck
once per turn, and pop a pendulum monster or scale you control
to spin another card on field back to deck without targeting.
Ignister Prominence and Luster Pendulum would over the coming months
take their positions as the poster boys for what became known as the pendulum pile deck,
which at this stage was starting to take form,
but wouldn't become an actual presence in the meta until the next core set.

Infernoid would see its third wave of support here in `Infernoid Decatron`,
who was the first normal summonable Infernoid,
able to dump an Infernoid on summon to gain its level, name and effects,
`Infernoid Tierra`, a fusion of Onuncu, Devyaty and any number of other Infernoids,
that gains effects for the number used to fuse her:
ripping both players' extra decks for three for three,
milling both players three for five,
returning three banished cards for each player to deck for eight,
and ripping both players' hands apart completely for 10,
and `Void Imagination`, a continuous spell that makes all Infernoids level one,
cuts Infernoid battle damage in half,
and, if the opponent controls an extra deck summoned monster,
lets you fusion summon an Infernoid using up to six materials from deck
in addition to hand and field, by sending itself to grave.
Put bluntly, the support was incredible for Infernoid,
giving them a monster that could actually be summoned proactively without setup,
and a way to get multiple monsters on the board,
with all of it plugging the deck's primary flaw.
Tierra and the fusion effect of Imagination were usually just seen
as a way to set up the grave for Infernoid pushes,
not necessarily for the actual benefit of the fusion summon,
but they would see play as well.

The Performages would see their introduction here,
a series of Spellcasters aimed at manipulating life points by their effects
to generate advantage.
`Performage Damage Juggler` could be discarded to negate and destroy a card
that deals effect damage, can be discarded to block the next battle damage
you take that turn, and can be banished from grave to search for a Performage.
`Performage Hat Tricker` can special summon itself
if there are two or more monsters on the field,
can gain a Performage counter to block effect damage dealt to yourself,
and its attack and defense become 3,300 when it reaches three counters.
`Performage Trick Clown` can summon a Performage from grave when sent to grave,
setting its attack and defense to zero and burning the user for a thousand.
And `Performage Trapeze Magician` is a Spellcaster-locked rank four
that blocks all damage less than its attack,
can detach to make a monster able to attack twice that turn,
but it blows up at the end of the battle phase,
and if destroyed, special summons a Performage from deck.
While unassuming on the surface,
Performages would become an engine that would shape the next year of the game,
as the advantage you generate solely off of Trick Clown would be enough to consider them useful,
but add in the damage blocking and searching of Damage Juggler
and the easy summon ability of Hat Tricker,
and it led the engine to see substantial play,
especially near the end of the year with another discovery from this same set.

We also received two new TCG exclusive archetypes to take the reins from BA and U.A.,
which would be present for the next four core sets.
The first of these was Kozmo,
a series of low level Psychics and high level Machines,
loosely based on a combination of The Wizard of Oz and Star Wars,
with the Psychics all sharing an effect to banish themselves
to summon a higher level Kozmo on a quick effect,
and the Machines all sharing an effect to banish themselves from grave on destruction
to special summon a lower level Kozmo from deck.
`Kozmo Farmgirl` could pay 500 upon dealing battle damage to search a Kozmo card,
`Kozmo Goodwitch` could pay 500 to flip a monster face-down,
`Kozmo Sliprider` pops a spell trap on summon,
and `Kozmo Forerunner` has targeting protection
and gives its controller a thousand life points back in their standby phase.
Lastly, `Kozmotown` was their field spell,
able to add a banished Kozmo to hand by paying life for its level times 100,
can shuffle any number of Kozmo monsters in hand to deck to draw that many,
and, if destroyed by card effect, searches a Kozmo card.
While not as explosive out the gate as Burning Abyss had been,
Kozmo was clearly going to be a powerhouse in future metas,
with this initial wave of support bringing `Emergency Teleport`
back to the forefront of player consciousness,
thanks to Farmgirl being a valid summon target with it,
and Forerunner being a super easy to summon boss monster
that was actually shockingly difficult to out with its targeting protection.

That targeting protection, though, would see a new way to out it
through the other new TCG exclusive archetype in Kaiju,
a series of high power monsters that can be summoned to your opponent's field
by tributing a monster they control,
and to your field if the opponent controls a Kaiju,
with each having an additional effect revolving around Kaiju counters.
`Dogoran, the Mad Flame Kaiju` can remove three counters to nuke your opponent's monsters,
`Kumongous, the Sticky String Kaiju` can remove two counters
to negate the effect of a newly summoned monster and block it from attacking that turn,
and `Kyoutou Waterfront` gains a counter any time a card is sent from field to grave,
capping out at five,
and can search a Kaiju once per turn if it's at three or more counters,
able to remove a counter to prevent effect destruction.
While next to no one would use the field spell or an actual Kaiju focused deck,
Kumongous would see play out the gate
for being able to tribute over an opponent's monster, a la `Lava Golem`,
without eating your normal summon that turn,
giving players an option to out Towers-like bosses,
specifically using Kumongous because it was the weaker of the two,
to give the opponent sitting at 2400 attack.

Moving into the one-offs.
`Magical Abductor` gets a counter whenever a spell is activated while on field or in scale,
able to remove three to search a pendulum monster while in scale,
or a level one Spellcaster while on field.
`Archfiend Eccentrick` can pop itself in scale to pop another spell trap on field,
or tribute itself on field to pop another monster.
Both of these cards would see experimentation in the aforementioned pendulum pile,
with Eccentrick seeing general play as well,
for being effectively an MST that could also double as monster removal where needed.
`Retaliating "C"` can special summon itself
if your opponent activates a spell that can special summon a monster,
banishing any card sent to grave while summoned this way,
and searches an EARTH Insect on being sent to grave.
While it's clear this was meant as a counter to strategies like Nekroz and Shaddoll,
Retaliating would also be extremely notable at the time
for searching `Maxx "C"` when removed.
`Wavering Eyes` pops all pen scales,
then provides effects based on how many you pop,
with one burning the opponent for 500, two searching for any pendulum monster,
three banishing a card on field, and four searching another copy of Wavering Eyes.
This was clearly meant as a counter to pendulums as they became more and more powerful,
but in actuality, it just acted as a way for pendulum strategies
to get an Igknight style search off,
which would become even more powerful if you matched into a pendulum strategy yourself.
`Chicken Game` blocked whichever player is losing from taking damage,
allowing both players on their turn to pay a thousand to use one of its effects,
being a draw one, give the opponent a thousand life, or popping that card.
Obviously the primary usage of this card was a pseudo reverse `Upstart Goblin`,
paying 1,000 life points to draw a card,
but this would become one of the most degenerate FTK enablers ever,
as the previously released field spell `Pseudo Space` could be placed over it
after using the draw effect,
to then banish it from grave, pay a thousand and draw again.
Because of this, Chicken Game FTK became a known threat in the format,
being utilized with either a `Magical Explosion` end goal or Exodia,
which was made worse by cards like `Hope for Escape` and `Terraforming`
enabling the strategy to insane degrees.
`Storming Mirror Force` would be the first in a new series of elemental retrains
of the classic battle trap,
bouncing all attack position monsters to hand when attacked into.
While this wouldn't see play for now,
it would set the trend for the next year of new Mirror Force variants coming out
with wildly different conditions and effects
that would each find their own homes in the game.

Lastly, and probably one of the biggest sleeper hits of the set,
Gem-Knight would receive two new cards of note.
`Gem-Knight Lady Brilliant Diamond` is a fusion of any three Gem-Knights
that can send a Gem-Knight from field to grave
to summon another from extra deck, ignoring its summoning conditions.
This enabled all sorts of FTKs with Lady Lapis Lazuli and `Gem-Knight Master Diamond`,
but for the time being was too inconsistent to do anything noteworthy.
The far more impactful piece of this support here was `Brilliant Fusion`,
a fusion-from-deck spell for Gem-Knights
that had no restrictions on when it could fuse,
but dropped the monster it summons to 0/0,
able to discard a spell to boost that monster back to normal stats for the turn.
While incredibly unassuming for the time,
Brilliant Fusion would actually be one of the most powerful setup tools
ever conceived for the time,
as by playing a copy of `Gem-Knight Garnet`,
you could use it to send the Garnet and any LIGHT to make `Gem-Knight Seraphinite`,
who in turn provided an additional normal summon,
as well as dumping of whatever LIGHT you wanted.
The go-to LIGHT of the era was undoubtedly Performage Trick Clown,
as by dumping it with Brilliant, Trick Clown could then revive itself immediately,
giving the user two monsters and an additional normal summon to work with
off of the one Brilliant Fusion,
which would become more and more popular in the coming months,
but was not immediately discovered on release.
This would also lead to the community coining the term "Garnet"
for anything you played in deck for an engine but did not want to draw,
named for the Gem-Knight Garnet you'd play,
as Brilliant Fusion had to specifically send its material from deck to grave.

#### World Championship 2015

The World Championship for 2015 would be held a week later, on August 15th, 2015,
and an extremely important note here is that the Worlds winner from 2014,
Sehabi Kheireddine of Canada, would not be allowed to participate despite his Worlds title,
due to a cheating suspension that was in effect at the time,
seeing his spot for the tournament filled by the runner-up of last year's Worlds,
Shunsuke Hiyama of Japan.
Once again, due to the combined format,
a number of strategies were left in an unplayable state going into Worlds.
Burning Abyss was still a TCG exclusive, leaving it out of the tournament completely,
while Shaddoll had taken many hits in the OCG,
with the banning of Construct and the limiting of `El Shaddoll Fusion`,
leaving the deck heavily suboptimal for play here.
Of the publicly available top eight lists, HERO would be a notable standout,
seeing top eight utilizing `Mystic Tomato` of all things,
in the slimmed down metagame, to access Shadow Mist for Dark Law,
with a suite of revival traps to make the setup even easier.
Nekroz would make it all the way to second place here,
despite the additional hits of a limited Unicore and a semi-limited Cycle,
utilizing more of the non-ritual Nekroz line than ever before,
like `Exa, Enforcer of the Nekroz` and `Great Sorcerer of the Nekroz`,
even going so far as to utilize the Hands once again
to fill out the control core of the deck.
Despite how he qualified for the event,
Shunsuke Hiyama of Japan would take this year's World Championship title
using Tellarknight,
the one Duelist Alliance strategy that had taken no substantial hits,
utilizing its almost standard game plan at this stage to consistently control games
and earn this year's title of World Champion.
This would lead into the next structure deck of the year, a week later,
and with the world substantially shocked by Tellarknight's performance on the world stage,
many hoped for something to help bolster a new meta strategy.

### Synchron Extreme Structure Deck

- Release date: August 28th, 2015
- Set type: structure deck
- Major strategies: Synchron
- Impact: new Synchro tools, nothing else

Synchron Extreme was a structure deck of all time for sure,
as while it didn't bring anything to affect the short term,
it did bring one tool that would affect the game in the long run in `Jet Synchron`,
a level one tuner that searches a Junk monster when sent to grave for a Synchro summon,
and can revive itself by discarding a card, and banishes itself when it leaves the field.
Jet may have been a little bit ahead of its time, as for now it wouldn't be too useful,
but a self-reviving tuner would be greatly appreciated
with the right amount of time passing.

#### YCS Toronto

YCS Toronto would be held the same weekend,
and with it there came a new format vibe in the air.
It was the first testing grounds of CORE, the first White YCS of the new season,
and with it would mark the changing over of the YCS prize card once again,
and for those still reeling from the stint of `Number 106: Giant Hand` a few years ago,
it was about to get so, so much worse.
`Minerva, the Exalted Lightsworn` can detach one to mill three,
drawing cards equal to the Lightsworns milled this way,
and when destroyed, mills three to pop cards up to the number of Lightsworns milled.
This card was, to put it bluntly, one of the best milling tools we've seen
since Lavalval Chain.
Being a rank four meant that it was so generically accessible
and boosted multiple strategies in the format,
from Shaddoll to Infernoid to countless rogue options,
and the fact that she was a prize card stirred up quite the storm,
because of her status as a prize card while also being legal in the format.
Copies of Minerva that you could potentially win at a YCS
were going for upwards of $1,000 a copy in her prime,
being the first time in a long time a prize card was worth nearly that much,
outside of specifically a Worlds prize card.

With all of that preamble out of the way, how did the meta for the YCS shake out?
For the most part, the meta held despite the new releases,
but a couple of clear shifts had made themselves known immediately.
For starters, the Performages were running rampant:
over half of the Nekroz lists, and most of everything
that wasn't specifically named Burning Abyss, were playing the clowns,
and the results here were clear that Performage was an engine that was here to stay,
providing rank fours and tribute bodies left and right.
Yang Zing would see its first ever premier top here,
utilizing the new Zefra Yang Zings from Crossed Souls
to provide the extra searching power for their Creation lines,
giving the strategy far more consistency than before.
Infernoid would quickly adapt to running Decatron,
but would take some time before the realization of power that was Imagination,
but given time would adjust.
Kozmo would be the big shock here, scoring a top cut appearance in its first ever outing.
The primary ability it was showcasing at this stage
was the ability to access any part of the core
through a combination of Sliprider and Kozmotown:
because Kozmotown searches on any card destruction effect, including your own,
it would be common practice to use Town's recursion, pop it with Sliprider,
and then search another Town, then activate it to do it all again,
since it lacked a hard once per turn clause.
This, combined with Forerunner being shockingly difficult to out,
allowed the deck to see decent success for its first rodeo,
and left many players looking to the deck for future outings.
Gabriel Orosan-Weine would take the event on Burning Abyss,
utilizing a newer tech in the deck, being Absolute King Back Jack,
as the way to combo with Draghig,
able to stack a Burning Abyss card,
then use Back Jack's effect to either mill a specific BA name
or set up both Fire Lake and the Traveler lines to absolutely blow out the opponent.

#### YCS Rimini

YCS Rimini would be held the same day,
and we'd see similar results in terms of Performage presence,
with Performages finding their way into most of the non-BA builds in the top 32,
including the winning Nekroz list piloted by Lorenzo Santoni.

#### YCS Monterrey

YCS Monterrey would be the following week,
and we'd see similar results to the other YCSs in this block,
even with the Back Jack build of BA once again taking the event,
piloted by Alan Santacruz Guzman.

#### 2015 Mega-Tins

The year's Mega-Tin would drop a week later,
and to say that the anticipation was palpable would be an understatement,
as this particular round was set to reprint a massive amount of the current meta.
Archetypes seeing reprints here included Artifact, Burning Abyss, Fluffal, Infernoid,
Qliphort, Shaddoll, "Sven", Tellarknight, Traptrix and Yang Zing, to name a few.
Needless to say, this would bring down the price of everything in the meta
not named Nekroz,
but there was also a new particular card here, brought in for the promo slots,
that absolutely needs to be talked about.
`Elder Entity Norden` is a fusion of two Synchro or Xyz monsters
that revives a level four or lower on summon.
Let's be blunt: at this point in time, you're never fusion summoning this card
the correct way.
You are absolutely using this with `Instant Fusion`,
and as such, Norden provides a get out of jail free card to every rank four focused deck,
by turning Instant Fusion into a one card rank four with its revive,
leading the card to see staple play in any rank four focused deck
for the foreseeable future.
This would lead nicely into the next set, two weeks later,
that was poised to take another crack at the deck building set formula.

### High-Speed Riders

- Release date: October 2nd, 2015
- Set type: deck building set
- Major strategies: Speedroid, Resonator, PSY-Frame
- Impact: new engines to tinker with

High-Speed Riders would be the second crack at the concept of a deck building set,
bringing three new archetypes to the table to drop massive waves of support for,
with two being brand new and one being new support for a legacy archetype.
The legacy archetype in question was Resonator,
the building blocks of Jack Atlas's `Red Dragon Archfiend` strategy,
seeing new tools like `Red Resonator`,
who summons a level four or lower from hand on normal,
or boosts your life points by a monster's attack on special,
but none of their pieces would convert the strategy into anything worth playing.

Speedroid would be the first of two new archetypes,
a series of WIND Machines aimed at swarming, level modulation,
and accessing a variety of Synchros in their toolbox,
like `Hi-Speedroid Kendama`, `Hi-Speedroid Chanbara`
and the previously released `Clear Wing Synchro Dragon`.
However, most of these pieces were lackluster and clunky, save two specifically.
`Speedroid Terrortop` can special summon itself if you have no monsters,
searching another Speedroid on summon,
and `Speedroid Taketomborg` can special summon itself if you control a WIND,
and can tribute itself to special summon a Speedroid tuner from deck.
These two would form the basics of a rank three engine for strategies at the time,
thanks to Terrortop searching Taketomborg for the immediate free summon,
giving you rank three material without committing to the normal summon,
seeing play mostly in decks like Burning Abyss as now a free access to Dante.

The last archetype here was PSY-Frame,
a series of Psychic gears that could special summon themselves
in response to an opponent's action if you control no monsters,
as well as the vanilla `PSY-Frame Driver`, banishing both in the end phase.
These included `PSY-Framegear Alpha`, who responded to a summon
and searched a PSY-Frame card on summon,
`PSY-Framegear Beta`, who responded to an attack,
popping the monster and ending the battle phase,
`PSY-Framegear Gamma`, who responds to monster effects,
negating it and destroying the monster,
`PSY-Framegear Delta`, with the same effect but for spells,
and `PSY-Framegear Epsilon`, with the same effect but for traps.
Their field spell, `PSY-Frame Circuit`,
allows you to immediately Synchro summon using the summoned materials regardless of turn,
with the primary targets being `PSY-Framelord Zeta`,
a level seven who can temp banish itself and an opponent's attack position
special summoned monster, able to recur a PSY-Frame in grave
by returning itself from grave to extra deck,
and `PSY-Framelord Omega`,
a level eight that can temp banish itself and a card in the opponent's hand,
can return a banished card to grave during the opponent's standby phase,
and can return itself and another card from grave back to deck.
While some experimentation would be seen with the various parts,
the only one that would see any success in the short term would be Omega,
being a generic eight with the ability to hand rip,
though some experimentation would be seen with the gears as hand traps,
specifically in the case of Gamma,
though this would go nowhere for the time being.

#### YCS Dallas

YCS Dallas would be held that same weekend,
and a surprising new face would be seen among the decks in the top cut,
being Clown Blade, a combination of the popular Performage engine
with various rank four enablers such as Assault Halberd and Thousand Blades,
with Thousand Blades specifically being interesting,
as its grave revive effect can trigger off the summon of Trick Clown.
The other interesting card here would be Gigobyte, in its first real playability,
thanks to Performages all being Spellcasters,
which in turn rose the usage of the rank four King of the Feral Imps,
which would only rise further and further in usage
as rank four toolbox decks fully take over.
Eric Christensen would take the event on Infernoid,
notably going completely undefeated throughout the event with an impressive 16-0-0 tear,
utilizing the newly released Omega for its hand rip,
as well as its ability to remove levels from the board for further Infernoid summons.
This would lead us into the last core set of the year,
and if it was anything like CORE before it,
it was sure to bring a shakeup to the meta as we knew it.

### Dimension of Chaos

- Release date: November 6th, 2015
- Set type: core set
- Major strategies: D/D, Majespecter, Kozmo
- Impact: the guillotine to the current meta

Dimension of Chaos would be the last core set of the year,
and with its new archetypes and continued support of themes introduced back in CORE,
a couple of new meta threats would rise from this particular set
to redefine the meta at large.

D/D is an archetype of Fiends aimed at utilizing Fusions, Synchros and Xyz
through their various tools and Dark Contract continuous cards,
which we've actually seen a couple of times before,
but this would be their first major wave of support
that would make the strategy playable.
`D/D Swirl Slime` can fuse a D/D/D using itself and any other material in hand,
and can be banished from grave to summon a D/D from hand.
`D/D Necro Slime` can fuse a D/D/D by banishing itself and any other material from grave.
`D/D Savant Kepler` searches a Dark Contract
or bounces another D/D card to hand on summon.
`D/D/D Oblivion King Abyss Ragnarok` summons a D/D from grave on summon,
and can tribute another D/D to banish an opponent's monster,
and in the scale can summon a D/D from grave when you special summon another D/D,
burning yourself for a thousand when you do.
`D/D/D Wave King Caesar` can detach at the end of the battle phase
to summon back as many destroyed monsters as you can,
but burns yourself for a thousand for each in the next standby phase,
searching a Dark Contract when sent to grave.
`D/D/D Wave Oblivion King Caesar Ragnarok` can bounce a D/D or Dark Contract on attack
to steal an opponent's monster and equip it to himself, boosting by its attack.
`D/D/D Duo-Dawn King Kali Yuga` can stun all other cards on field for the turn on summon,
can detach to nuke all spells and traps,
and can detach to set a Dark Contract from grave.
Dark Contracts are all continuous cards with similar conditions
of providing an effect once per turn
and burning the controller for a thousand in the user's standby phase.
`Dark Contract with the Gate` lets you search a D/D,
`Dark Contract with the Swamp King` lets you fusion summon a Fiend using hand or field,
and can also use grave if the monster being summoned is a D/D,
and `Dark Contract with the Witch` lets you send a D/D or Dark Contract from hand to grave
to pop a card on field, and boosts all Fiends by a thousand on the opponent's turn.
D/D would look interesting from this wave,
but unfortunately was missing quite a lot of its important support
to do anything relevant for the time being,
as the remainder of their critical support was still locked in the OCG as exclusives
needing to be imported,
so the deck's viability was going to live or die by how soon we'd see those pieces.

Performage would actually see a couple of new pieces of pendulum support.
`Performage Mirror Conductor` can swap a monster's attack and defense
and take 500, or, if in scale,
drop a special summoned monster's attack or defense to the lower of the two,
and `Performage Plushfire`, if destroyed, floats into another Performage in deck,
and in scale can summon itself if your Performage is destroyed,
then you take 500 damage.
These two were seemingly harmless additions to the lineup at first,
but a realization hit with Plushfire specifically:
if it was, say, destroyed in the pendulum scale,
it would be a monster again in the extra deck where it ended up.
This means that by popping Plushfire in the scale
with something like Luster Pendulum or Wavering Eyes,
it would still trigger its float effect,
and with that, pendulums now had full unrestricted access to the Performages
in a way no other deck did.
This would heavily increase the interest in the aforementioned pendulum pile,
now slowly gaining the name of the two archetypes it most utilized,
those being Performages and Performapals, nicknamed to Pepe for short.
It seemed that with every new release, some new pendulum cards would be added to the pile
to make Pepe stronger and stronger,
which actually segues really nicely into Majespecter.

Majespecter was a new archetype of Spellcaster pendulums
that all shared targeting and effect destruction immunity,
and having no pendulum scale effects despite being a pendulum archetype,
with most having an effect on summon,
with `Majespecter Cat - Nekomata` searching any Majespecter card in the end phase,
`Majespecter Raccoon - Bunbuku` searching a monster,
`Majespecter Crow - Yata` searching a spell, and `Majespecter Fox - Kyubi` searching a trap.
`Majespecter Unicorn - Kirin` would be the boss of the archetype,
able to bounce a pendulum monster and an opponent's monster to hand on quick effect,
though notably was a level six in a pendulum archetype where the scales capped out at five.
Their spell trap line primarily involved tributing a WIND Spellcaster to activate an effect,
with `Majespecter Storm` spinning a monster to deck,
`Majespecter Cyclone` popping a monster, `Majespecter Tornado` banishing a monster,
and `Majespecter Tempest` negating a special summon or monster effect, destroying it.
Lastly, `Majesty's Pegasus` was the deck's field spell,
boosting all Majespecters by 300
and letting you tribute a WIND Spellcaster to summon a Majespecter from deck.
The fact that the entire archetype had targeting protection,
on top of Kirin being an absolute annoyance,
would lead them to see meta playability, not just in their own pure variant,
but also as additional pieces to Pepe,
thanks to Bunbuku and Kirin together forming a self-contained package of monsters,
similarly to Igknight.
Though they also had an extra deck monster
that tied the archetype into the Dracoslayer lore,
being `Majester Paladin, the Ascending Dracoslayer`,
a rank four that searches any pendulum monster in the end phase after being Xyz summoned,
and can detach to summon a Dracoslayer from the face-up extra deck,
forming a pseudo loop in combination with Ignister Prominence,
and instantly becoming a staple piece of Pepe thanks to being a rank four.

Speaking of Igknight, Igknight would also receive a couple more pieces here,
in `Igknight Cavalier` and `Igknight Veteran`, finishing out their sets of level fives and sixes,
as well as `Igknight Reload`,
which shuffled back any number of pendulum monsters in hand to draw that many plus one,
with Reload seeing not only play in Igknights,
but experimentation in different pendulum piles.

Graydle was a new archetype aimed at stealing opponent's monsters,
with most of their main deck monsters equipping to and stealing an opponent's monster
when destroyed in battle or by a monster effect for `Graydle Eagle`,
spell effect for `Graydle Alligator` or trap effect for `Graydle Cobra`.
`Graydle Slime` could summon itself from hand or grave by popping two Graydle cards,
summoning a Graydle from grave when you do,
providing easy access to their Synchro boss, `Graydle Dragon`,
able to pop cards up to its Synchro materials on summon,
floating into another monster in grave on destruction.
Lastly, `Graydle Impact` lets you pop a Graydle card and an opponent's card,
and searches a Graydle in each end phase, only able to use one of those effects in a turn.
Graydle, while seemingly clunky, actually held some interesting applications,
thanks primarily to the Kaiju from CORE,
able to tribute over a monster, then steal the Kaiju with a Graydle,
seeing some experimentation in that aspect.

Speaking of, Kaiju would receive `Gameciel, the Sea Turtle Kaiju`,
now the weakest Kaiju at 2200 attack,
making it the new go-to counter option for outing untargetable bosses,
and that would become even more relevant with the new wave of Kozmo support.
`Kozmo Strawman` had the standard pilot tag out,
and could pay 500 to summon a banished Kozmo with its effects negated,
destroying it in the end phase.
`Kozmoll Wickedwitch` had the standard pilot tag out,
and could pay a thousand to prevent herself from being destroyed that turn on a quick effect.
And `Kozmo Dark Destroyer` had the standard ship float effect, targeting protection,
and popped a monster on summon.
These three would take the already tier three Kozmo strategy and rocket it up to tier one,
as Dark Destroyer was an absolute house of a card,
with the only real way to out it being that of the Kaiju,
as popping it would just let it float into another ship or pilot
that could just get back to it.

On to the one-offs.
`Performapal Secondonkey` searches a Performapal on summon if your scales are full,
being a decent inclusion in Pepe as it geared up.
`Fluffal Wings` allows you to convert a `Toy Vendor` into pure value,
drawing a card and popping the Vendor, which in turn gets you another search.
In the same vein, `Frightfur Sabre-Tooth` is a fusion of any Frightfur fusion
and one or more Fluffal or Edge Imps,
summons a Frightfur from grave on fusion summon, boosts Frightfurs by 400,
and has destruction immunity if made with three or more materials.
Sabre-Tooth would be seen as effectively a freebie fusion using Tiger,
since you could just revive them after,
allowing you to either make a large bodied board between their attack buffs,
or potentially clear the Tiger off the board for another Tiger summon.
`Vector Pendulum, the Dracoverlord` could negate the effects
of the opponent's pendulum scales while in scale,
being a decent option for the pendulum pile,
but still lacked a solid reason to run it, which we would get soon enough.
`Pot of The Forbidden`, on flip, gave you the choice of effects
from power spells that are currently banned,
be it Pot of Greed draw two, Giant Trunade spell trap bounce,
Raigeki's monster wipe, or Forceful Sentry's hand spin back.
Obviously this was too clunky to see meta contention,
but it was experimented with for the rogue level play
with the previously mentioned Prediction Princess cards,
as Tarotrei could use it as her ritual material, then bring it back in the end phase,
able to flip it up and down repeatedly.
`Odd-Eyes Fusion` fusion summons a Dragon using monsters you control,
or, if the opponent has two or more monsters than you,
Odd-Eyes monsters from the extra deck.
This would facilitate the new boss of `Odd-Eyes Vortex Dragon`,
a fusion of an Odd-Eyes and a pendulum monster that bounces a monster on summon
and has an omni negate at the cost of returning a pendulum from the extra to deck.
This would be solid for some pendulum piles to include,
thanks primarily to `Odd-Eyes Rebellion Dragon`,
which, with two copies of it in the extra deck,
can be used to fully meet the fusion requirements,
since Odd-Eyes Fusion doesn't specify face-up extra deck, seeing some experimentation.
`Scarlight Red Dragon Archfiend`'s name becomes the original Archfiend on field and in grave,
and can nuke all other special summoned monsters with equal or less attack to it
to burn the opponent for 500 for each.
This would become a quick inclusion in the ever growing lineup of good level eight Synchros,
primarily as a way to close out games effectively.
`Painful Decision` lets you dump a level four or lower normal
to search another copy of the same monster.
While fairly basic on paper, at this stage the only deck that could even remotely
consider using it was Igknight, though it would see relevance within the next year.
Lastly, `Grand Horn of Heaven` can negate an inherent special summon
in the opponent's main phase, then immediately force them to end that phase.
This would see some serious experimentation as a blowout option when going first,
as forcing the opponent to effectively skip their main phase 1
can prove detrimental in terms of breaking a board,
as you lose your battle phase to go into main phase 2 to finish up.

#### YCS San Jose

YCS San Jose would be the same weekend,
and Kozmo would waste no time letting everyone know who was the new top dog,
taking the main slice of the top cut and the event itself, piloted by Jesse Samek.
The addition of Dark Destroyer and Strawman to the deck had changed the dynamic substantially,
as now you could do combos with the deck,
by utilizing Dark Destroyer's pop to cycle your ships in the battle phase
for further damage pushes or to break the opponent's board,
while Strawman's ability to access a temporary beater body
that then floats into more power proved extremely useful.
Reasoning had found another home here too,
as almost every ship and pilot was a different level,
giving no ideal call outside of eight to prevent a free Dark Destroyer drop.
Majespecter would also clear into top 16 here,
utilizing the other pendulum tools like Luster Pendulum and Archfiend Eccentrick
to take full advantage of the Majespecter lines,
in addition to Moon Mirror Shield being quite useful,
as with the targeting and effect destruction protections on the Majespecter lines,
it made the smaller bodies incredibly difficult to out without something like a Kaiju,
which always felt like a waste on the small pendulum bodies.

#### Ban list (November 9th)

This would be the precursor to the next ban list update just three days later,
and with it would come down the blade on almost everything
that had been meta in the past year.
Newly banned were `Apoqliphort Towers`, `El Shaddoll Construct`, "conru",
`Evilswarm Exciton Knight` and Shurit,
all of which had been pivotal pieces of the decks that had dominated the game for a while now.
Newly limited were `Graff, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss` as a hit to BA,
Mathematician and El Shaddoll Fusion as hits to Shaddoll,
Nekroz of Brionac and Nekroz of Unicore and `Reinforcement of the Army`,
all as hits towards Nekroz,
Qliphort Scout as a hit to Qli, and Ritual Beast Ulti-Cannahawk as a hit to Ritual Beast,
all of which were massive nerfs in their own ways.
Newly semi-limited, and the only card here, would be `Cir, Malebranche of the Burning Abyss`,
as another hit to BA.
Lastly, unlimited were `Chaos Sorcerer`, Shi En, `Spellbook of Fate` and `Geargiagear`,
seemingly a wave of cleanup to the decks of yesteryear.

At this point, Shaddoll, Qliphort, Ritual Beast and Nekroz all suffered killing blows,
losing almost their entire identity in one fell swoop,
while Burning Abyss, though it did take hits,
didn't suffer nearly as much as the others.
This would also spawn a meme in the community around Dante,
as it seemed that the BA poster child,
unlike the faces of the other meta strategies,
was ban list immune in the TCG.
Obviously this would set the stage for a changing of the guard in a sense,
and most expected Kozmo to move into the dominant position moving forward.

#### YCS Sao Paulo

This would be all but confirmed at YCS Sao Paulo three weeks later,
where Kozmo would take a solid nine of the top 16,
including first place, piloted by Alejandro Reyes Suarez,
marking the player's second YCS win and third major event win.
This would be the first major event top of Pepe as well,
but unfortunately we do not have a deck list to analyze for this particular event,
though Pepe was about to become much more potent in the meta,
as the last structure deck of the year was just a week away,
and it would provide a preview of the coming attractions
that pendulum had in store for 2016.

### Master of Pendulum Structure Deck

- Release date: December 4th, 2015
- Set type: structure deck
- Major strategies: Performapal, Odd-Eyes, Pendulum Magician
- Impact: the precursor to the clown show

Master of Pendulum would be the final set of the year,
and with it would come one of the biggest bursts of support we had seen yet
to Pepe specifically,
with the support here breaking down into three archetypes,
so let's go at it one at a time.

Performapal support would be the least in number but one of the largest in power,
thanks to `Performapal Skullcrobat Joker`,
who on normal searches a Performapal, Pendulum Magician or Odd-Eyes monster,
becoming an instant staple for the deck thanks to accessing any of the three engines on command.

Odd-Eyes would receive a new field spell in `Sky Iris`,
which blocks your Magicians, Performapals and Odd-Eyes scales from being targeted,
and lets you pop a card to search for an Odd-Eyes once per turn,
and two new extra deck bosses in `Odd-Eyes Meteorburst Dragon`,
who specials a pen scale on summon and locks the opponent's monster effects
in the battle phase,
and `Odd-Eyes Absolute Dragon`,
who can detach to negate an attack and summon an Odd-Eyes from hand or grave,
and, if sent to grave after being Xyz summoned,
summons an Odd-Eyes from extra deck, ignoring its summoning condition,
with Sky Iris finding a home instantly,
and Absolute taking one of those Odd-Eyes Fusion slots,
thanks to also being a cheat out if needed.

Lastly, the Pendulum Magician archetype would see a serious facelift.
`Dragonpit Magician` and `Dragonpulse Magician` were normal pendulums
that could discard a pendulum to pop a spell trap for Pit or a monster for Pulse.
`Oafdragon Magician` could return a Magician or Odd-Eyes from face-up extra to hand
once per turn.
`Wisdom-Eye Magician` could pop itself in scale to set any other Magician into scale from deck.
And `Pendulum Call`, for the cost of a discard,
let you search for any two Magician pendulums,
locking your pen scales from being destroyed by card effect
until the opponent's next end phase.
This was an incredible boon for the rapidly rising Pepe,
seeing a variant known as Pepe Magicians rise into the meta off of this wave.

#### YCS Lille cancellation

Which I would say we'd see at the last YCS of the year in Lille,
but there was an unexpected issue:
due to devastating attacks in Paris, coupled with civil unrest,
YCS Lille would sadly be cancelled.

### Outro

Leaving us now at the end of 2015 Yu-Gi-Oh, but what a massive year it was.
We had seen the rise and fall of a new tier zero,
as well as the changing of the guard in terms of meta threats,
and with recent support, finally we were seeing pendulums come into their own as a mechanic.
But this was about to hit the fan with the beginning of 2016 on the horizon.
The clowns were about to get more and more powerful,
and with it we'd see an approach Konami would take that we've never seen before or since
to help keep the chaos in check.
